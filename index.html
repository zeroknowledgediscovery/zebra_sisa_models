Discussion with Trudi Matthews, UK HealthCare · October 19, 2026

Open with the headline. ZeBRA stands for Zero-Burden Risk Assessment: it screens for near-term suicidal ideation and attempt risk using only data already in the record. No tests, no questionnaires, no patient contact. It still detects risk in patients with no prior depression diagnosis (AUC 0.81–0.85) and even with no mental or behavioral diagnosis on record at all (AUC 0.70–0.76, the strictest test).

The same ZeBRA/ZCoR approach is peer reviewed for dementia (npj Digital Medicine 2026), outcomes after acute pancreatitis (Pancreas 2026, in press), pulmonary fibrosis (Nature Medicine 2022), autism (Science Advances 2021) and post-surgical cardiac risk (JAHA 2022); links are on the slide.

Introduce Julie Cerel as the suicide-prevention expert on the team: Professor in the UK College of Social Work, director of the Suicide Prevention & Exposure Lab, past president of the American Association of Suicidology, and developer of CODE RED universal safety planning used in Kentucky schools.

Then thank Trudi and give the purpose: we'd value her advice on how this could be tested in practice at UK HealthCare, and later statewide.

Kentucky's age-adjusted suicide death rate (17.5 per 100,000 in 2023) is well above the US rate (14.1). Hospitals recorded 44,372 suicidal-ideation diagnoses in 2023, partly because universal ED screening began that year. Medicaid pays for 60% of suicide-related ED visits, which matters for value-based contracts.

The ~9% figure is our illustrative Lexington VA calculation: 167 documented SI/SA reports in FY2024 against ~1,851 expected at a conservative 5% latent prevalence. The point is that current reporting sees only the tip of the iceberg.

The blind spot

Risk builds up in the record long before anyone asks

No single code is a red flag. ZeBRA reads the pattern across diagnoses, prescriptions and procedures that are already recorded, and flags risk before anyone has asked.

Illustrative timeline. Risk factors named in the manuscript include trauma, substance use, sleep disturbance, chronic pain, neurological injury and patterns of health care use.

Why passive surveillance works. Screening sees a patient a few times a year and depends on what they disclose. Meanwhile the record keeps accumulating ordinary codes: a head injury, an opioid or sleep prescription, an ED visit, a pain clinic visit, an antidepressant change.

None of these is alarming on its own. ZeBRA learns the combinations that precede a first recorded suicidal ideation or attempt and flags the patient about a month ahead. This timeline is illustrative, not a real patient.

How ZeBRA works

ZeBRA reads risk from data the health system already collects

Routine records

Diagnosis, medication and procedure codes, with dates

ZeBRA model

Learns risk patterns across more than 43,000 codes

Threshold alert

Fires when risk crosses a threshold set to local capacity

Care team action

Outreach, direct risk assessment and safety planning

No questionnaires
No clinical notes
No new visits
No new data entry

Uses the same structured codes recorded for routine care and billing, and returns a risk score through a lightweight API.

Same platform, peer reviewed: autism (Science Advances, 2021) · pulmonary fibrosis (Nature Medicine, 2022) · post-surgical cardiac risk (JAHA, 2022) · Alzheimer's and related dementias (npj Digital Medicine, 2026) · acute pancreatitis outcomes (Pancreas, 2026).

Plain-language version: every visit leaves a trail of diagnosis, prescription and procedure codes. ZeBRA learns the longitudinal patterns in those trails that precede a first recorded suicidal ideation, self-harm or suicide attempt code. When a patient's score crosses a threshold, the care team gets a time-bounded alert.

Nothing new is asked of patients or clinicians to generate the score. The same ZeBRA approach has been published for autism, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, post-operative cardiac events and, most recently, Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (npj Digital Medicine, July 2026) and outcomes after acute pancreatitis (Pancreas, 2026, in press). That dementia paper included a prospective feasibility pilot in which ZeBRA scores tracked MoCA cognitive scores (R = -0.78), a useful precedent for prospective work.

Evidence · National validation

29.6M

0.87–0.91

AUC one month ahead, across four sex and age models

≈1.0

A positive screen raises near-term SI/SA odds 11–13-fold.

All results on held-out patients not used in training (Merative MarketScan).

Retrospective cohort of 29,568,802 patients aged 25–75 (Merative MarketScan, 2008–2024), with separate models for men and women aged 25–50 and 50–75. At the primary 1-month horizon AUCs were 0.87, 0.89, 0.90 and 0.91. At 95% specificity the positive likelihood ratio was 11.3–13.0: a flagged patient's odds of a near-term SI/SA event are 11–13 times their pre-screen odds. Calibration slopes were 0.96–1.00 and Brier scores 0.003–0.006, so the risk scores can be read as real probabilities when thresholds are set.

Evidence · High-risk and hidden groups

It detects risk even without a prior psychiatric diagnosis

Signal beyond known psychiatric history

No prior depression diagnosis: AUC stays 0.81–0.85.

No mental or behavioral diagnosis at all: AUC 0.70–0.76. The model is not simply re-finding known psychiatric patients.

TBI and PTSD: strong performance in two groups at elevated, hard-to-predict risk.

Most screening is anchored on depression. Many people who later attempt suicide are never identified through depression-centered pathways. ZeBRA kept AUC 0.81–0.85 in patients with no prior major depression diagnosis, and 0.70–0.76 even when we excluded everyone with any prior ICD-10 mental or behavioral disorder code (F10–F69). TBI (0.88–0.90) and PTSD (0.82–0.83) performance matters for veteran and trauma populations.

Evidence · Timing

A positive screen opens an 8–10 week window for action

Observed in held-out MarketScan data (coded SI/SA prevalence 0.4–0.7%) at the 99%-specificity threshold; midpoints across four sex–age models. Not a UK HealthCare projection.

~50%

of flagged patients had a documented SI/SA event within 8 weeks (55–60% by 10 weeks)

>50 pts

absolute risk difference vs non-flagged patients at 10 weeks, in every sex and age group

95–98%

of patients below threshold stayed event-free over the same window

An alert is a time-bounded trigger for review, not a permanent label.

This is what makes the alert operational. After a patient's score crosses the 99%-specificity threshold, the probability of a documented SI/SA event rises from about 16–18% at 1 week to 30–35% at 4 weeks, about 50% by 8 weeks and 55–60% by 10 weeks. Patients who never cross stay 95–98% event-free. In Kaplan–Meier terms, the 10-week absolute risk difference exceeded 50 percentage points in all four groups (log-rank p < 10-20).

So the care model is: alert, then focused outreach over the next 8–10 weeks.

Illustrative: FY2024 Lexington VA counts, 5% latent prevalence; not a formal cost-effectiveness analysis. The manuscript's 0.6% (Supplement A) counts only false-alert review costs.

Illustration using Lexington VA: 37,017 unique patients in FY2024, 167 documented SI/SA reports, ~1,851 expected at a conservative 5% latent prevalence. At 99% specificity ZeBRA would flag ~944 patients: ~592 true and ~352 false (PPV ~63%), more than tripling identified cases. At 95% specificity: ~1,055 true and ~1,758 false (PPV ~38%), capturing ~57% of the estimated burden.

Break-even: each alert costs ~$100 to review and each treated self-harm event ~$28,000, so triage pays for itself if 1 in 280 alerts (0.36%) leads to an avoided treated event. At 95% specificity that is ~10 avoided events across 2,813 alerts, about 1% of the 1,055 correctly flagged patients. The manuscript's 0.6% is a different ratio: false-alert review costs only, divided by true positives times $28,000. The threshold is a dial: UK HealthCare can choose the alert volume its teams can absorb.

Strategic fit

ZeBRA strengthens programs UK HealthCare already runs

Value-based programs

Fewer avoidable self-harm ED visits and admissions across Medicare, Medicaid and commercial contracts. Medicaid pays for 60% of Kentucky's suicide-related ED visits.

Complements Joint Commission suicide-prevention requirements (NPSG 15.01.01) by extending risk identification beyond behavioral-health encounters.

Health IT and statewide reach

Built on standard structured codes, a natural fit for Kentucky Regional Extension Center support to partner and rural practices.

Connect ZeBRA to what her office already owns. (1) Value-based care: avoided self-harm ED visits and admissions are exactly the utilization these contracts reward, and Medicaid is the dominant payer for suicide-related ED care in Kentucky. (2) Healthy Kentucky's QPR campaign trains people to ask the question; ZeBRA tells clinicians whom to ask. (3) Joint Commission NPSG 15.01.01 focuses on behavioral-health encounters; ZeBRA can surface risk in patients seen for anything else. (4) Because it needs only standard codes, it can travel through the Kentucky REC network to rural practices.

This is a straw-man for discussion, not a fixed protocol. The retrospective study cannot tell us (a) how ZeBRA performs on Kentucky patients, who differ from commercially insured MarketScan enrollees, (b) what alert volume a clinic actually sees, or (c) whether outreach triggered by an alert reduces SI/SA events. Phases 1–2 are low-cost and low-risk (no patient-facing change) and are the natural first ask. Phase 3 is the evaluable intervention that state and federal funders will want to see. Phase 4 is where statewide population health and rural reach come in.
